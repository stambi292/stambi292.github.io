Catching card fraud, priced in euros

A card issuer sees hundreds of thousands of payments a day and only a handful are fraud. I trained a machine learning model on 284,807 real card transactions and turned its output into the decision a bank actually makes: which payments to stop or review, and what that is worth.

Independent analysis by Stephen tAMBI on a public, anonymised dataset. Not affiliated with any bank or payment company.

Set the alarm, see the money

The model gives every payment a fraud score from 0 to 1. The bank chooses a threshold: payments above it are flagged for review. Lower thresholds catch more fraud but flag more honest customers. Results are for the model's test period, the last hours of the data it never saw while learning.

Why "99.8% accurate" means nothing here

99.83%

is the accuracy of a "model" that says no payment is ever fraud. It catches nothing. With fraud this rare, accuracy hides the problem, so I judged the models on precision-recall: how much fraud they find, and how clean their alerts are.

ModelPR-AUCROC-AUC

PR-AUC runs from about 0.002 (guessing) to 1.0 (perfect). Higher is better.

How it was built

  • Data: 284,807 card transactions by European cardholders over two days in September 2013, 492 of them fraud. The bank anonymised the features (V1 to V28 are principal components), so only time and amount keep their meaning.
  • Honest test: the models trained on the first 70% of the time window and were tested on the last 30%, the way a bank would use them: on future payments, not shuffled ones.
  • Models: logistic regression as a baseline, gradient boosting and a random forest, all weighted for the rare fraud class, in Python with scikit-learn. The random forest scored best and drives the page. The page's numbers all come from the test period.
  • Money: a caught fraud saves its amount, a missed one loses it, and every alert costs the review cost you set. Chargeback fees and lost customers are left out, so real savings are likely larger.
Data: Credit Card Fraud Detection dataset, Machine Learning Group of the Université Libre de Bruxelles (Dal Pozzolo et al.), published on Kaggle. Amounts are treated as euros, as the dataset comes from European cardholders. Independent analysis by Stephen tAMBI. Not affiliated with any bank or payment company.
